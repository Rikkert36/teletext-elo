The subject is the object, not the surface

This is the finding the whole drawing turns on, and it was arrived at the expensive way. The first versions drew the table faithfully: the surface with its markings — penalty area, goal area, the D, corner arcs, centre circle. Shown the result cold, without being told what it was, a reader called it “an association football pitch, seen from above — immediate and unambiguous”, and named exactly those markings as what gave it away.

They were right, and the fault was not in the drawing. A foosball table's markings are football's markings. Every one of them is a mark the thing it must not be mistaken for also has, so drawing them accurately could only ever produce a pitch. Fidelity to the reference was the problem.

So what is drawn is the furniture: walls with a thickness, rods threaded through those walls, men on the rods, handles out in the margin, serve holes, and a goal that is a slot cut through an end wall rather than two boxes painted on grass. A single hairline round a rectangle is a touchline; two lines 3.2 apart is the side of something. None of it is available to a pitch.

Every painted marking went with that decision, and then most of them came back — the halfway line, the centre circle, the penalty area, the goal area and the D. Taking them out was the right diagnosis and the wrong cure: without them the spread stopped being a pitch and became a wiring diagram, and a reader shown it cold said so. What changed in between is that the drawing now has the furniture to carry them. The object is what keeps it off a pitch; the markings are what keep it a table rather than a schematic. Only the corner arcs stay gone — they were pure football and bought nothing.

Where the paper is

A slots page shows bare paper in exactly three places: a sliver above the cards, the narrow gaps between them, and about 18% of the page height below the last row. That last band is the only generous one, and it is the whole composition problem — a mark not in it is a mark almost nobody sees.

The table sits the way up the reference has it: goal at the head, halfway line and centre circle at the foot. It was turned end for end for a while, on the argument that the goal is the least pitch-like mark in the drawing and so deserved the open band. That was right about the goal and wrong about the picture — the reference is a table seen from its own end, and turning it over is not a crop, it is a different subject.

It also gave up more than it bought, which the flip hid. At this crop the rod that lands in the band is the five-bar — so the open paper gets five figures, four of them well clear of the fold, on a rod running out to a handle in the margin. Turned over, that band held a keeper's single man sitting on the fold, where a reader took the figures for the album's own binding studs. The BOX height is solved for this: five-bar at 78% of the page, the top of the centre circle at 76% just clear of the last card, the halfway line at 87% above the folio, and the walls running off the trim at 99% so the cut never shows.

The fold is the field's long axis. The goal's centre and the middle man on every odd rod fall on x = 34, which is the fold. The two halves are not mirrors — the handles alternate, so each leaf carries a genuinely different half of one table — and it has to work that way, because leaves turn independently and nothing may span the gutter as one element. Both pages are handed the same marks and their viewBoxes do the cropping, split at 34.

Half a table, four rods

Half, because a spread is half. The four rods are the first half of the real 1-2-3-5-5-3-2-1 order, at the real spacing — the surface is 120 × 68, so this is 68 across by 60 down and every figure below is in those units rather than in pixels. It can be checked against a tape measure instead of against taste.

The left/right alternation is the real one: a rod belongs to whoever's side its handle comes out on, which is why the 3-bar in the middle is the opponent's and reaches in from the other margin.

The men are solid, every one of them. They were outlines, and the outlines lost: a reader listing every mark they could make out on the page did not see a single figure, and called their absence “the single decisive omission — bare horizontal lines with no beads on them are pitch rules, not rods.” A figure on a rod is a small dense mark, and at watermark weight an outline of one is nothing at all.

Solid for one side, outlined for the other. The reference draws one team's figures black and the other's white, and on ivory paper an outline with the stock showing through is the white one — so the opponent's 3-bar comes out light against three dark rods, exactly as it does on the table. This was tried once and abandoned, back when both teams were outlines and nobody could find a figure on the page at all. The lesson was that an outline alone is nothing at watermark weight, not that the distinction was wrong: against solid neighbours an outline reads perfectly well. Solid is the default and the outline is the exception, which is the way round it has to be.

And they are figures rather than beads. An oval on a rod is a bead; a shape with a head, a waist and a kicking foot is a man — and it is the waist doing the work, because a shape pinched exactly where a line passes through it reads as threaded onto that line. The outline is a table of ten half-widths in FIGURE, walked down one side and back up the other so it closes and hand can stroke it like any other primitive.

Drawn, not plotted

The first version of this was exact SVG geometry, and exact was wrong. A perfect vector diagram on aged stock reads as a screenshot of something else pasted under the page; what the album wants is the drawing a printer would have made for it. So every line here is stroked by hand: each primitive is a parametric curve sampled with a smooth wobble along its own normal, laid down twice with different phase, and corners overshoot the way a hand's do.

The low frequency is what carries it. The first version ran all three wobble terms at 1.6 cycles and above, so a line 400px long picked up several small ripples that averaged back out to straight — at 1:1 it read as plotted, and the hand was visible only on the little grip rectangles, where one ripple happened to span the whole side. A hand does not ripple along a rule; it bows away once and comes back. The dominant term is now 0.7 cycles over the whole primitive, barely half a wave, with two faster terms as texture on top. Raise the amplitudes if it needs more hand; never the frequencies.

It is the same argument the checklist ticks already won — three drawn paths rather than a glyph, hashed from the player id so the mark is stable. The wobble here is seeded per element from a fixed table, so the drawing is identical on every render and on every page. A field that re-sketched itself when the leaf turned would be an animation, not a watermark. The Hand slider above is a tuning control only; it is a constant in the real thing.

Everything is the press's brown #6b5325 and not --graphite: the two-hands rule on the checklist page says graphite is the reader's, and the reader did not draw this. It came off the press with the paper. An earlier version hatched the end wall, the way a section is hatched in a technical drawing; it was the loudest mark on the spread and the only one the reference has nothing like, and it went.

What is still open

The ink is not the lever it looks like. The slider starts at .30 and that is about right: at this weight the drawing is unambiguously subordinate to the cards in every region and never competes with the silver or the gold. When a mark cannot be read the answer is almost never to turn this up — it is that the mark is behind a card. Every fix worth making moves marks into open paper.

The men on the fold. Three rods carry a figure at the exact centre of the table, which is the fold, so three ovals sit down the gutter in the shadow. It is physically honest and it is not obviously right: shown the page cold, one reader took them for the album's own binding studs. The alternative is dishonest — a keeper is centred — so this is recorded rather than fixed.

The side walls hide under a card column. At the current scale the two wall lines fall at 47% of each page, which is the middle of a card, so for most of their length they surface only as short stubs in the row gap. Solving the scale so they land in a column gap instead is the next real move, and it retunes every vertical number in BOX.

The tab. The selector runs the candidates: a spread number, which is the one figure nothing else on the page states and is honestly true of both halves; the rating range, which the head already says four centimetres away; and the folio, which the foot already says. Only the first says something new, and it is still a second piece of furniture on a page that was deliberately emptied.

What it costs. Two <svg> per spread, built once from a table of four rods and about twenty marks, at z-index: 0 under .album__slots. No library — the wobble is thirty lines of parametric sampling, which is well inside the rule that keeps framer-motion and friends out of this project.
