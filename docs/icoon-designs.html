Icoon cards

Five proposals on Nynke, each shown beside the bronze, silver and gold cards exactly as they are today — plus the answer to the "should they all be gold" question. Everything sits on the real card geometry from src/styles/card.css: same masks, same type, same cqw ratios. What you see here is what lands in the album.

First: what an icoon is in this system

Icoon is not a fifth tier. Icoon is the legend. That is not a way around docs/trading-cards.md saying "There is no Icoon tier" — it is what the reference does too. A FIFA Icon does not sit above gold on the same axis; it is a different axis: who someone is (a retired great) rather than how good they are now. That axis already exists here — legends are inactive players with ≥10 games, rated on their all-time high. So "icoon" is simply the better word for what the card already calls isLegend.

What it replaces is the black legende pill, which is currently the only element on the whole card that is a label rather than material. That is exactly the reasoning that removed every plated version of the number and the name; the pill never got put through it.

For the same reason, none of these prints the word "icoon" on the card. Doing so would be the pill again in nicer type. Everything else on this face — the number, the name, the tier — is unlabelled, and the legend flag is no different: if the material does not say it, a caption will not rescue it.

Effect on rarity: none. An icoon is not a separate draw, not extra tickets, not a new threshold. It is the existing legend flag finally doing something on the face. ticketsFor(), DHigh, drawPack() and the whole odds table are untouched.

Gold ink

The reference sets the number, the name and every rule in a warm bronze-gold rather than a dark ink, and that is most of why those cards read as pressed rather than printed. The Ink button in the bar at the top switches every icoon on this page between the two.

Two things have to move together or it does not work. The type goes metal and the name divider goes with it — a gold number over a black hairline is just a card with a gold number on it. And the engraved highlight under that rule has to warm up too: a white highlight under a gold rule reads as a gold line lying on white paper rather than as a line cut into a warm card.

One brown across all three colourways, not a bronze per metal. The first pass gave each colourway its own ink and it was wrong twice over: it made the ink a second thing the tier moves, when the whole colourway rule is that the tier moves the metal and nothing else — and three inks that are all nearly the same brown is a distinction nobody can make while being three things to keep in step.

It is also a brown rather than a gold — #594D2C. A saturated gold competes with the metal it is printed on and loses, because the ground is already the gold. What the reference sets its type in is nearer a dark olive-bronze, which reads as ink that happens to be metallic rather than as more metal. The divider is that same colour at 62%, so the two cannot drift apart if the ink is retuned again.

Left of each pair is the dark ink, right is gold. Watch the contrast at album size before deciding: the number is the only hard fact on the card, and gold on ivory has a good deal less of it than near-black on ivory.

The border, and where it sits — dropped, kept as a record

This section is history. The frame was taken off in the end (see Settled: E, frameless), but it took five passes to arrive at a version worth rejecting, and the reasons are worth not repeating. Everything below still works from the Edge toggle in the bar at the top.

Three passes were spent on this and all three failed the same way, so the settled part is worth stating flatly:

  • The gold sits on the artwork. Nothing is inset or cleared to make room for it. An earlier attempt stopped the photo at the frame and reserved an empty band, and that is a mount holding a picture — not what the reference is.
  • There is no stock outside the gold at all. The inset went 3.4% → 2.6 → 1.7 → 0.85 → 0 over five passes, and every intermediate value was wrong the same way: any stock left outside the gold reads as a pale margin around a frame rather than as the card being framed, and the eye finds it however narrow it is. There is no width at which a margin stops looking like a margin — there is only having one or not having one.
  • So the ring's outer edge is the card's outer edge, and it takes the card's own 6px radius. The two are now the same curve rather than two concentric ones.

The rails are not the same width all the way round — thick along the top (1.05cqw), ordinary down the sides (0.78cqw), thin along the bottom (0.6cqw). That is what a bevelled frame lit from above looks like: the top rail catches light across its whole face, the bottom one is turned away and nearly edge-on. It also solves a rendering problem, because a uniform hairline landed on 0.75px at album size and was anti-aliased into grey.

The metal gradient runs bright at the top to dull at the bottom, and only in that direction. It used to end on a bright stop, so it lit up again at the bottom corners and dimmed in the middle — light arriving from two places at once, which is the one thing that kills a frame.

The gold thinning out at the top-left and bottom-right was never width. It was tone. The metal started at a near-white cream and the ivory stock it is drawn on is also near-white, so at the lightest end of the ring there was no contrast to see a width with. Two fixes: no stop in any metal is now lighter than the stock it sits on, and the ring casts a short contact shadow. It is a drop-shadow rather than a box-shadow because a mask clips box-shadows away entirely while a filter is computed from the masked silhouette — the same reason the pack wrapper's shadow is a filter.

The two readings, side by side

What the reference shows for certain is two gold lines close together, near the edge, drawn on the marble. What it does not settle is whether that is one object or two:

  • Beaded moulding — one piece of gold beading, catching light on both of its rounded edges with a dark groove down the middle. Three contiguous rings; no card stock anywhere inside the assembly. The dark line is a shadow.
    The groove is wider than either bead (--groove at 1.4cqw against a 1.05cqw top rail). At its original 0.45cqw it read as a seam where two lines meet rather than as a gap between two objects. The beads stay equal to each other: making the inner one heavier thickens the frame without deepening it, which is the wrong axis.
    It is a warm brown, not a black. Taken to near-black it stopped being a shadow in gold and became a slot cut through the card. A shadow is the absence of whatever light is falling, and the light here is warm — so gold in shade is brown, not grey.
    Each bead is dark on both sides, which is what makes them read as round rather than as flat gold stripes. Three of the four edges already had it; the inner bead's inner side had nothing, so it was lit on one side and open on the other. No amount of widening the groove could fix that — it is on the wrong side of the bead. That edge now casts onto the card, hardest from the top where the light is.
  • Twin rules — two independent hairlines with clean card stock between them. A wider gap, and what fills it is the card. The dark line does not exist.

My read of the crop is beaded. The two below are pinned; everything else on the page follows the Edge toggle.

The proposals

A against E, on four faces

A and E differ by exactly one layer and nothing else. Same ivory ground, same frame, same photo grade, same type. E adds a fan of light shards radiating from a point below the card — and that is the whole difference. If you cannot see it, that is not you missing something subtle; it is the finding.

The reason it hides: the shards sit behind the photo, so they can only show through where the portrait mask has cleared it — the bottom strip and the top-left corner. The first card below is E with the photo stripped away, so you can see what the layer is before hunting for it on a finished card.

Four players at their real overalls and their real tiers. The second pair in each row is the same two cards with the frame off — the gold band is the loudest thing on the card and it is identical on A and E, so it drowns out the one layer that actually differs.

Settled: E, frameless

The frame is out. Five passes went into getting it to sit right, and the version that finally did is the one that made the card worse — it was the loudest thing on the face, it was identical on every proposal, and it flattened the difference between them. The reference needs a frame because its card is a shaped silhouette with a crown and shoulders, and gold piping is what draws that shape. Ours is a 5:7 rounded rectangle, so the piping had no shape to draw and became jewellery.

What is left is the thing that was carrying the idea the whole time: ivory ground, monochrome photo warmed back up, and the shards. And with the frame gone the shards finally read — they were never weak, they were being shouted over.

Each player twice: their ordinary card as it stands today, and their icoon.

The tier still moves the metal. Nynke and Jeroen are silver icoons, Daan and Laura gold ones — same structure, different ground and shard colour. That decision is unaffected by dropping the frame; if anything it matters more now, because the ground is doing all of the work the band used to share.

Considered and rejected: touching the 84/85 line

The two golds stay exactly as they are. Recorded here because the argument against them is superficially good and will otherwise be made again.

It goes: the 75–84 and 85+ cards differ only in tone, tone is a comparison you can only make with both cards adjacent, and there are 6 rare golds scattered among 34 slots — so in the album the distinction is invisible. Therefore give rare gold something structural instead.

It does not survive, for three reasons.

  • The rarity signal was never the metal. Tiers are cosmetic labels; the hard fact is the overall printed in the corner, and the thing that actually announces a big pull is the ceremony, which steps at 75 / 80 / 85 / 90.
  • The reference agrees. Gold rare and gold common in FUT are barely distinguishable too, deliberately. A subtle difference is not a failed loud one.
  • It is expensive in the wrong place. Spending a structural signal on rare gold is the one thing that would blunt the icoon, which is the card this page is about.

75–84 and 85+ as they are, with an icoon at the end. The gap that has to be legible at album scale is the last one — and it is, without touching the two before it.

Also not proposed: a fourth state at 90. The ceremony steps there, but that is one player today, and a visual state you see once is not a state.

Should every icoon be gold?

No — but the tier may only move the metal, never the structure.

The reason is arithmetic rather than taste. Legends are rated on their all-time high, and that spreads. The placeholder pool in cardMock.ts runs from 1690 down to 845, which is overall 88 down to 72 — rare gold through to silver. A real inactive player who managed ten games and peaked at 780 is an icoon at 69. If every icoon is gold, that card lies about him, and the number in the top-left — the only hard fact on the face — stops agreeing with the material around it. That is precisely the mistake .card--zilver once made with its white tint.

  • Structure constant — ground, photo grade, shards. Four cards, one object.
  • Metal per tier — exactly the three --tier-* properties the card already uses, plus two shard colours. No new machinery and no new tier in tierFor(): card--icoon is an extra class alongside card--zilver, not instead of it.
  • Bronze stays rare, and that is fine. An all-time high below 800 means a short career. A colourway you almost never see is what a colourway is for.

The same card, three metals. Silver and bronze cool the metal only; the wash over the photo stays warm, because a cool one cancels the sepia and leaves a grey silver card rather than an icoon.

In context

On teletext black. The ivory variants win hardest here: they are the only light object in the entire album.

On wood. B falls over here — against a warm ground the difference from an ordinary gold card almost disappears — and C holds up best.

Recommendation

E — Shard, frameless, with the three colourways.

Three parts and no more: an ivory ground, the photo graded to monochrome and warmed back up, and one fan of shards from a point below the card. One conic-gradient, so no asset and one extra div in PlayerCard.

Runner-up: C — Obsidian, and not weakly. It is the only one equally strong on all three grounds, and it rhymes with the wrapper and the card back. The objection is that it will not bend to the tiers: black-with-silver and black-with-bronze are the same object with a different accent, where E genuinely is three metals.

Do not ship B. A gold card with a sepia photo is a gold card, and in the album — where icoons lie among rare golds — you cannot tell at a glance.

Cut: F — Marble. A literal reading of the reference's stone, built as three feTurbulence layers. Two things killed it. The veining read as cloud rather than crack at card size, and more fundamentally the stone can only appear where the portrait mask has cleared the photo — so it framed the picture instead of sitting behind the figure, which is the whole point of it in the reference. That needs cut-out portraits, which is not a CSS problem.

The change is small and purely additive: an isLegend branch adding card--icoon card--icoon-e cw-{tier} in PlayerCard.tsx, one <div class="card__shards"> beside the portrait, the .card__legend pill deleted, and one block in card.css. No new tier, and nothing touched in tierFor(), drawPack() or the ceremony.

Still open: the shaped silhouette. The reference's card is not a rectangle, and that is where a lot of its character lives — but it is also why it needs the gold piping we just dropped. Changing the card's outline touches the album grid, the pack opener's FLIP and every drop shadow in the feature, so it is a separate decision.
